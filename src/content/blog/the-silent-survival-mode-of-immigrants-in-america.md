Before anything else, I want to name this clearly: this essay will not reflect every immigrant's experience. Our stories are layered, contradictory, and shaped by very different losses and gains. What follows is not an attempt to generalize or reduce them. It is simply a reflection of patterns many of us have lived, witnessed, and carried quietly while building lives far away from where we began.

## Living Between Two Worlds

One of the hardest parts of the immigrant experience is not choosing between countries. It is living inside the space between them.

Many of us genuinely admire America. The opportunity. The freedom. The respect for different kinds of work. The sense that life can expand here in ways it could not before. That admiration is real.

And yet, alongside it sits something heavier. A quieter cost we do not often say out loud.

We arrive and slowly realize that diaspora communities often hold onto versions of "home" that no longer exist. Meanwhile, the countries we left behind continue to evolve without us. Over time, we begin to feel out of sync everywhere. Not fully here. Not fully there. Not fully who we once were. We live in a middle space with no clear reference point, carrying identities that no longer fit neatly anywhere, including inside ourselves.

Layer onto that realities of visas, job dependency, and the pressure to succeed quickly and visibly. The fear of instability. The unspoken knowledge that leaving a job is not just a career decision but a survival one. Add the guilt of wanting more ease than we have, and the quiet fear that admitting dissatisfaction might make us seem ungrateful.

And then there is another truth that complicates this story even further.

For many of us, leaving home did not feel like a loss. It felt like relief.

Some of us grew up in family systems or cultural environments that were suffocating, restrictive, or emotionally unsafe. Distance gave us space to breathe for the first time. Being far away allowed us to grow independently, to meet parts of ourselves that had no room to exist before. But even that relief carries its own contradiction. We can feel liberated by distance and still feel unanchored by it. The place that once constrained us can continue to shape us. And the place that freed us can still feel isolating.

This is the split we rarely name. We can love the lives we are building here and still feel hurt by what they demand of us.

## Loneliness Beneath the Functioning

Immigrant loneliness is not always loud. Often, it hides behind competence.

It is the loneliness of holding two lives at once: the one we are building here and the one that continues elsewhere without us. Our vacations are not rest; they are reunions compressed into a few precious days. We miss weddings, funerals, ordinary celebrations, and everyday rituals that once made life feel grounded. Over time, absence becomes cumulative.

In a culture that prizes independence, community does not come easily. It has to be built slowly, intentionally, often while we are already exhausted. Many of us learn how to function exceptionally well while feeling deeply unheld.

Alongside this sits a constant, low-level anxiety. About visas. About work. About whether the sacrifices we made will ever feel fully worth it. We keep moving because slowing down feels dangerous. We stay productive because it is what we were taught to do. External success becomes a kind of armor, something that protects us from having to acknowledge how depleted we feel underneath it all.

From the outside, we look settled. Inside, many of us are running on empty.

## Is There a Way Out of This Feeling?

The honest answer is complicated.

This feeling may never disappear entirely. It may soften. It may change shape. It may become easier to carry. But it may not vanish. That is not a failure. That is the reality of belonging to more than one place, and never fully to just one.

What can change is how tightly this heaviness grips us.

For many of us, steadiness does not come from resolution. It comes from permission. Permission to admit that this is hard without immediately trying to fix it. Permission to miss home without wanting to return. Permission to imagine another life without betraying the one we are living. Permission to evolve without freezing ourselves in the expectations we once carried.

Support, in whatever form feels safe, matters. Not because we are broken, but because carrying this much alone was never meant to be sustainable.

None of this erases the duality. It simply makes it livable.

Many of us will always walk with one foot in each world. The goal is not to choose between them. The goal is to build a life that feels like ours, even if it is complicated, even if belonging never feels clean, even if the longing stays.

Some things are not meant to be resolved.

They are meant to be carried with more gentleness.
